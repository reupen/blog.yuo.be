I saw that there was going to be a partial lunar eclipse in the early morning on
Friday, 28 August 2026. I couldn’t remember the last time I’d seen a lunar
eclipse. Good ones don’t come particularly often, so I thought that I may as
well try and photograph it.

My first problem was that the moon was going to be low in the sky, so I needed
somewhere with an unobstructed view. I walked around my local area trying to
scout out a good spot. I thought a road facing south-west in a slightly hilly
area might be a good option. Alas, that particular one wasn’t – there were still
trees visible near the horizon (perhaps it was facing another hilly area).
Ignoring the trees, I’d still have had to stand in the middle of the road and,
even then, would only have a narrow view without obstructions from houses. It
turned out, though, that I should’ve looked closer to home – there was a good
view on my own road that I hadn’t appreciated just by looking at a map.

My next problem was whether the sky would be free of clouds. Several sources
were predicting significant cloud cover in my location. On the night before,
however, the Met Office was forecasting clear skies at the time of the eclipse –
both overhead and in the direction of the moon. So I was cautiously optimistic
that there’d be a clear view of the lunar eclipse.

I set my alarm and got up in the middle of the night and had a look outside. The
Met Office was right – there was a clear view of the moon. I picked up my tripod
and camera kit and set up outside. I knew that the low altitude of the moon
would make shooting conditions more challenging (due to my line of sight going
through more of the atmosphere). I hadn’t, though, properly considered how much
difference the darkness of a lunar eclipse (in comparison to a normally
illuminated moon) would make. I had set up my Canon camera with an RF 800mm F11
IS STM lens and an RF 1.4x Extender. And I could not see the eclipsed section of
the moon (the part that was in the earth’s
[umbra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbra,_penumbra_and_antumbra)) on my
camera’s display at all. I quickly ditched the extender (widening the aperture
from ƒ/16 to ƒ/11 as a result), increased the ISO speed significantly and
increased the exposure time. I still couldn’t see anything other than the small
bright portion of the moon on my camera’s screen.

I was tired, so I just kept shooting, hoping for the best and adjusting settings
slightly between bursts of continuous shooting to give me more of a chance of
capturing something with optimal settings. I then went back to sleep, leaving
looking at the photos to normal waking hours.

When I got to looking at my photos, I was disappointed. After applying an
exposure boost, I could make out some of the eclipsed part of the moon. But it
was extremely noisy. Nonetheless, I attempted to stack the photos I’d taken,
hoping that it would get rid of the noise (or at least reduce it to an
acceptable level). It took a fair bit of effort to get something okay-ish out.
This is what I ended up with:

There is, unfortunately, a lack of shadow detail. There is also a diagonal noise
pattern – I assume this is camera sensor noise moving relative to the moon in
the frame between shots (I tried hard to get rid of this noise during stacking
but was unsuccessful). On the plus side, the moon covers a good number of
pixels, and its bright section isn’t overexposed.

That stack was taken at approximately 05:01 BST (11 minutes before maximum) when
the moon was at an altitude of +9.5°. The stack was based on 75% of a set of 101
photos, shot at a 1/160s exposure time, ISO 2500 and the fixed ƒ/11 aperture of
my lens.

The next few lunar eclipses in London don’t look overly interesting to me. The
one on 20 December 2029, however, is a total lunar eclipse and will be at an
altitude of around 58°. It’s a bit of a wait, but perhaps I’ll be able to get a
better shot of that one.
