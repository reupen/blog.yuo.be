Last year, I made a
[rudimentary attempt to capture some Perseids on video](/2026/01/16/a-couple-of-perseids-from-2025/)
from my London home. Inspired by the fact that I managed to get something, I
decided to try again this year.

I was better prepared this time. I knew what camera settings I used on my Canon
EOS R5 Mark II last time, so I didn’t need to spend much time thinking about
those. And I was ready to record video at the peak of the shower, rather than
two days later.

And so I set up my camera, just after midnight, pointing it at a largely
unobscured area of the sky near-ish the
[radiant](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiant_(meteor_shower)>). I picked a
22mm focal length to strike a balance between getting a good amount of the sky
in view without the individual meteors being excessively small. I knew the
battery in my camera would run out after around an hour of recording, so I
decided to connect it to a power bank. I still thought that the camera would
overheat and stop recording at some point. In reality, while the camera did heat
up, it kept on recording indefinitely (and the power bank lasted a lot longer
than I thought it would). I eventually stopped the camera manually at 4am.

I saw some meteors while the camera was recording, so I was hopeful I had
captured something (while also worrying that I had somehow messed something up
when setting the camera up and captured nothing). I noted the current recording
time whenever I saw a meteor with my own eyes, so I could check those moments in
the video later.

To my relief, the meteors that I saw were indeed in the captured video, plus a
fair few more. Here’s a montage of meteors from the recording:

That’s 36 meteors from 3 hours and 46 minutes of footage. That’s probably more
than I thought I’d get from my location (and there were some trees partly in
view – with hindsight, I’d have pointed my camera higher than I did).

There could be some additional meteors in the original recording that I haven’t
found, but they’d probably be very dim. I did also see some stretched bright
blobs in a single frame of the video that look like meteors to me, but those are
hardly worth looking at.

I should also point out that one or two of the meteors in the above montage
don’t appear to originate from the radiant of the Perseids, and so they
seemingly aren’t actually Perseids.

Would I do this again? Well, there would be little point in doing the exact same
thing again. Trying to get some better footage would make things more
interesting, though.

A better lens (with a wider aperture than the
<span className="f-stop">ƒ/2.8</span> that I used here) would let me use a lower
ISO speed and get a less noisy video. And travelling somewhere less
light-polluted than London would of course get better results, but it also all
becomes more effort and less convenient.

For this video, I used the XF-HEVC S YCC422 10bit and 4K-DCI Fine settings on
the camera. The highest resolution the camera can do is 8K-DCI, and it can also
do RAW video recording. Getting such a long recording with those would be
impractical though, as the camera is likely to reach its thermal limit and shut
down (if my memory card doesn’t fill up first, that is). Still, it may be worth
experimenting to see what recording mode actually gets me the most detail in the
night sky (and I can probably do that just by pointing the camera at some
stars).
