I’ve been to Japan twice. But I never thought to try any fancy Japanese fruit
while I was there.

As it turns out, though, I don’t need to take a 14-hour flight to do that, as
you can get hold of expensive Japanese fruit in London. And I like mangoes, so I
thought I’d give some Japanese mangoes a try.

So, I recently picked up a gift box of two Taiyō no Tamago Miyazaki kanjuku
mangoes.

<CaptionedImage
  src={box_top_avif}
  alt="The red presentation box that the mangoes come in."
  caption="The rather nice presentation box"
/>

Taiyō no Tamago (太陽のタマゴ) means Egg of the Sun. Miyazaki (in hiragana
as みやざき on the packaging) refers to the prefecture where the mangoes are
grown. Kanjuku (完熟) means fully ripened. The Public Relations Office of the
Japanese government has
[an English article about Miyazaki mangoes](https://www.gov-online.go.jp/eng/publicity/book/hlj/html/202402/202402_05_en.html).

I found a good deal (compared to, say, Harrods) on the mangoes at an importer
that is a distributor but also sells direct to the public. (Perhaps that should
be ‘sold’ rather than ‘sells’, as that importer mysteriously seems to have
deleted all their online presence. Maybe they regretted selling the mangoes to
me that much?)

I picked the mangoes up in person (saving a bit compared to having them
delivered, and also saving the mangoes from spending a day with a courier).

Getting back to the box, the side of it has some stamps:

<CaptionedImage
  src={box_side_avif}
  alt="The side of the presentation box, with various blue stamps."
  caption="I see stamps, I check what they mean"
/>

The first column says the grade (等級) of the mango is shū (秀, roughly
translating as ‘excellent’). Digging a bit online, it seems shū is split into
red (aka-shū) and blue (ao-shū) sub-tiers, with red being better than blue. (My
mangoes were blue, it looks like.)

The middle column says that there are two mangoes inside that are size 4L. (The
‘L’ here seems to mean large, making 4L something like the fourth level of
largeness. The size is actually determined by weight, and 4L seems to equate to
roughly between 510 and 649g.)

The last stamp names the grower of the mangoes.

On lifting the top half of the box, I was greeted by a leaflet:

If you were wondering what the text on the far-left page in the photo says,
here’s a (largely automated) translation:

> Among Miyazaki fully ripened mangoes, only those that meet the strict criteria
> of a sugar content of 15 degrees \[Brix\] or higher and a weight of 350g or
> more are shipped as ‘Taiyō no Tamago (Egg of the Sun)’.

> Thank you very much for purchasing this Miyazaki fully ripened ‘Taiyō no
> Tamago’ mango.
>
> Please chill the mango well in the refrigerator before eating. Its flavour is
> truly worthy of its reputation as the ‘Queen of Tropical Fruits’, offering a
> rich, complex taste with the fragrant aroma of the tropics. We are confident
> that you will be thoroughly satisfied with it.

The QR code [leads to a website](https://www.sampleweb.jp/mango/) with
instructions for two different cutting methods and some recipes.

<CaptionedImage

Those pages provide a bit of information on how the mangoes are grown, and
contain cutting instructions.

Lifting the top foam padding revealed the mangoes. They were a luscious deep
red:

<CaptionedImage
  src={box_mangoes_avif}
  alt="The mangoes in the box, each in some net padding for protection."
  caption="The mangoes looked comfortable where they were"
/>

The mangoes had a bit of condensation on them (presumably due to them coming out
of a fridge).

It might not be clear from the photo, but the mangoes were a good size. To give
you an idea of the size, the opening of the box measured 20.3 by 13.5cm, and,
excluding the foam padding, the cavity was about 9cm deep.

In that same photo, you can see the two stickers on each mango. The top sticker
says, ‘Taiyō no Tamago Miyazaki fully ripened mango’. The bottom one says,
‘Saito, Miyazaki Prefecture mango’ (referring to the
[city of Saito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saito,_Miyazaki)) and mentions the
[Japan Agricultural Award](https://agri.ja-group.jp/promote/prize) at the very
bottom.

The mango itself had a code, 09西都28010, on it:

<CaptionedImage
  src={mango_code_avif}
  alt="One of the mangoes, with the code visible."
  caption="The code is made up of little dots"
/>

I think it’s a production code of some kind. 西都 again means Saito.

One mango was slightly riper than the other, so I cut that one on the same day I
got the mangoes. You’ll have to excuse my mango cutting skills:

<CaptionedImage
  src={mango_cut_avif}
  alt="The mango cut into two, with one side scored into cubes."
  caption="I was missing the knife from the leaflet"
/>

(I suppose that wasn’t quite what I thought it would look like inside.)

What did it taste like? Well, it was delicious. Probably the nicest mango I’ve
had. If I were comparing the taste to other mangoes I’ve had, it was probably
most similar to some (air-freighted) tree-ripened Peru mangoes that you can find
in specialist independent grocers in the UK (when they’re in season).

Would I buy it again? No. It was nice, but that doesn’t justify spending that
much on mangoes. Still, it was nice to try it once.

Once the mango was eaten, a puddle of juice was left behind:

<CaptionedImage
  src={mango_gone_avif}
  alt="The juice left behind on a chopping board."
  caption="It was a juicy mango"
/>

The second mango went in the fridge, and I ate it the next day.
