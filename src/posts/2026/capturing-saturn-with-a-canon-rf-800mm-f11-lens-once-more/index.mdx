Almost three years ago,
[I had a go at capturing Saturn with a Canon EOS R mirrorless camera and a Canon RF 800mm F11 IS STM lens](/2023/11/07/jupiter-and-saturn-with-my-canon-camera/).
The camera combined with that lens doesn’t exactly make for proper
astrophotography equipment (the narrow ƒ/11 aperture is particularly
suboptimal), and so it was just a bit of fun to see what I could capture given
that I already had them in my possession.

Since then, I’ve upgraded to a Canon EOS R5 Mark II and I bought an Extender RF
1.4x. I’d also like to think I have a bit more of a clue about what I’m doing,
so I’ve been meaning to try again to see if I can get a better result. Saturn is
[approaching its closest point to Earth for the year](https://theskylive.com/saturn-info#distance)
– it seemed like a good time to make that new attempt.

When a cloudless night came along, I got up out of bed as Saturn approached its
highest point in the sky for the night and set up my camera outside on a tripod.
The combination of an RF 800mm F11 IS STM lens and the Extender RF 1.4x gave me
a focal length of 1120mm. With Saturn in frame on my camera, I took a series of
4-second bursts[^1] of RAW photos at 30fps using the electronic shutter and the
Canon Camera Connect mobile app.

In total, I took 23 bursts of photos, each with between 100 and 113 photos. I’m
not sure there was a massive difference between most of them. But in the end, I
selected a set of 113 frames shot at an exposure time of 1/250s, ISO 1250 and an
aperture of ƒ/16 (which was fixed due to the lens and extender combination).

This time, I mixed things up by using [Siril](https://siril.org) to do the
stacking. I used the development version, which has
[a new multi-point planetary registration and stacking feature](https://siril.readthedocs.io/en/latest/preprocessing/multipoint.html).
Siril can also read RAW images for my camera model (though it’ll convert them to
the [FITS format](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FITS) for processing), and has
some [demosaicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosaicing) algorithms[^2],
such as LMMSE, that aren’t normally available[^3] in
[LibRaw](https://www.libraw.org).

I didn’t demosaic my images during Siril’s initial conversion step and instead
let Siril handle it during stacking. As an illustration, though, here’s one of
the frames from the selected burst of photos, demosaiced by Siril:

I’ve brightened the image so that something is actually visible. Note that Siril
intentionally does not correct the white balance of the RAW image, leaving
Saturn looking greener than usual.

To get the registration step working, I had to brighten the frames. (I did this
linearly to avoid messing the processing up.) I set it to stack 75% of the
frames in the burst, which produced this:

<RawCaptionedImage
  src={stacked_1x}
  alt="A soft photo of Saturn with minimal noise. Saturn appears green and small."
  caption="The noise is mostly gone"
/>

The colours are obviously off. Also, though, the RGB channels are misaligned due
to
[atmospheric dispersion](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/astrophotography/atmospheric-dispersion-corrector#:~:text=What%20is%20atmospheric%20dispersion%3F).

I aligned the RGB channels using OpenCV and a Python script that I already had
and then used the Curves tool in [GIMP](https://www.gimp.org) to sort out the
other problems. Here’s what I had after that:

<RawCaptionedImage
  src={stacked_1x_adjusted_gimp}
  alt="A photo of Saturn in more natural colours. Saturn appears small in the frame."
  caption="What colour is Saturn, really?"
/>

It’s already looking better than my previous attempt in 2023. I hadn’t yet
sharpened the image, though. As usual, I used wavelet sharpening. I turned to
[waveSharp](https://codeberg.org/Corbee/waveSharp3.0) (which is no longer being
developed, it seems) for that. One reason I like waveSharp is that it lets you
sharpen only the luminance channel, avoiding exacerbating any chroma noise in
the image.

Here’s a slightly sharpened version of the previous image:

<RawCaptionedImage
  src={stacked_1x_adjusted_sharpened_gimp}
  alt="A photo of Saturn. Saturn is slightly sharper than the previous photo. Saturn appears small in the frame."
  caption="It’s a subtle improvement"
/>

In 2023, I used AutoStakkert! and its 3x
[drizzle](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drizzle_(image_processing)>) mode to
produce an image three times the size of the input frames. The multi-point
stacking mode in the development version of Siril doesn’t have a drizzle option,
but it does have a ‘Scale’ parameter with choices 1x, 1.5x, 2x and 3x. Here’s
how the parameter
[is described in the documentation](https://siril.readthedocs.io/en/latest/preprocessing/multipoint.html#stacking-parameters):

> When a value greater than `1x` is selected each frame is upscaled using OpenCV
> interpolation before contribution to the upscaled stack buffer. This is
> similar to the old "Simplified Drizzle" method provided for deep-sky stacking.

And here’s the result of using the 3x scaling option, cleaned up and slightly
sharpened:

<RawCaptionedImage
  src={stacked_3x_adjusted_sharpened}
  alt="A photo of Saturn. It is three times the size of the previous photo."
  caption="I can stop squinting"
/>

To be honest, simply resizing the previous smaller image (with a high-quality
algorithm) produces a more aesthetically pleasing result. I suppose there isn’t
much fine detail visible here where there might’ve been a more useful
difference.

Anyway, the result is a fair bit nicer than my 2023 attempt. Alas, it seems
unlikely that I’m going to be able to do much better without some more
appropriate equipment (like, a telescope…).

[^1]:
    The camera can only maintain 30fps RAW image shooting for around 4 to 4.3
    seconds, after which time it’ll need a breather while buffered shots are
    written to the memory card. Recording video is an option too that I haven’t
    tried for shooting planets (I can imagine dealing with the video formats
    produced by the camera would be a nightmare…).

[^2]:
    The camera uses a type of
    [colour filter array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_filter_array)
    called a [Bayer filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayer_filter) to
    produce a colour image. The pixels in the RAW image data are in an RGGB
    pattern and so [demosaicing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosaicing) is
    required to produce a normal colour image.

[^3]:
    The demosaicing algorithms supported by LibRaw
    [are listed in its documentation](https://www.libraw.org/docs/API-datastruct-eng.html#:~:text=0%2D10%3A%20interpolation%20quality%3A).
    Some additional algorithms are in the historical
    [LibRaw-demosaic-pack-GPL2](https://github.com/LibRaw/LibRaw-demosaic-pack-GPL2)
    and
    [LibRaw-demosaic-pack-GPL3](https://github.com/LibRaw/LibRaw-demosaic-pack-GPL3)
    repos (it seems the separate repos were for licensing reasons). (I say
    historical repos as the first one says it’s abandoned in the README, and the
    second one hasn’t been updated for 16 years.)
